Conclusion at a glance

What is established

The CZSO Compare Regions application uses 34 demographic, social, economic and environmental indicators; daily commuting time is not on their published list. Other frameworks may include it — the OECD has used it in the past as a secondary indicator. It therefore always depends on the specific methodology.

What remains uncertain · What would change the conclusion

What remains uncertain

The sources alone do not isolate every cause of the observed pattern or determine how it will develop when conditions change.

What would change the conclusion

Newer comparable data, stronger causal identification or documented results of an implemented measure would change the conclusion.

What we know: In the published methodology of the CZSO Compare Regions application there are 34 indicators, and commuting time is not among them. The CZSO does, however, track the time and mode of commuting in other data products, especially the 2021 Census. The OECD already used commuting as a secondary indicator in How’s Life? 2011.[1][6][22]

What we do not know: This text has not verified the current complete list and weights of every Czech commercial or local index. It therefore cannot claim that “no index” includes commuting. Nor does a population average say how much time a particular household spends travelling in 2026.

Wages, greenery, access to a doctor and time in a traffic jam are not interchangeable indicators. A district may have low unemployment and, at the same time, long commutes. An index may correctly add up its columns and still not measure an individual’s journey time at all. Regional GDP is moreover attributed to the place of economic activity, not automatically to the place of residence.

1. A ranking that does not see an hour in the car

Imagine a family meeting about moving. The father wants a district with a high median wage, because the table promises prosperity. The mother wants a nursery and a doctor within walking distance, because the children’s calendar is harder than a ranking. The mayor praises the motorway junction, because the investment is visible. The index analyst praises low crime and greenery, because those can be calculated. The doctor asks about blood pressure and sleep after years behind the wheel. The municipal accountant asks who will pay for the nursery for people whose GDP is counted a district away.

At first glance this is a dispute about where life is better. In reality each person is describing a different problem. The father compares income. The mother the accessibility of services in time. The mayor infrastructure. The analyst static territorial quantities. The doctor the burden on the body. The accountant tax revenue.

Each family member can therefore correctly describe a different part of everyday life, while the aggregate ranking itself determines none of these priorities. The phrase “quality of life” joins income, services, safety, the environment, journey time and individual preferences; choosing a house by a single score conceals those differences.

First it is necessary to determine which household need the comparison is meant to address. Pay, a park, a motorway junction and journey time answer different questions and require different data sources.

An index is not a life. A road is not accessibility. GDP is not income. And a district’s rank is not your afternoon.

2. What is hidden behind a single ranking

When a gold medal for a district appears in an infographic, it is easy to form the impression that that is where life is best. The result, however, is produced in a chain. Each layer can raise the score, lower it, or change the type of error. Two indexes with the same word “quality” therefore need not measure the same thing, and the same district can behave differently according to whether we read the supply of services or the time a person must travel to reach them.

Five layers of one score

  1. Supply in the territory: wages, doctors, nurseries, greenery, safety — what indexes can do.
  2. Accessibility in time: minutes to work, school, the clinic, not kilometres on a map.
  3. Mode of travel: car, public transport, walking; each comes with a different cost.
  4. Body and sleep: a burden that does not fit into an ORP.
  5. The account and the tax: where GDP is created and where people live.

Specific Czech products must be read separately. In the Compare Regions application the CZSO uses 34 published indicators from the demographic, social, economic and environmental domains; commuting duration is not on this list.[1] The Ministry of Regional Development and Obce v datech work with different territorial units and their own sets of indicators.[2][3] Without the current complete methodology and weights it is not honest to generalise their content. International frameworks also differ: the OECD has eleven well-being dimensions and already in 2011 included commuting as a secondary indicator.[5][22] The precise conclusion therefore reads only: the specific CZSO Compare Regions set does not measure individual journey time; it is not true that no framework measures it.

Without a cut through the layers, praise for a district misses the day. That cut determines where to turn in a dispute. If a district shines and people get up at 5:15, the time layer may have failed, not the wage layer. If a station exists and the service does not run at shift time, accessibility has failed, not the existence of the track. The sentence “life is good here” is the start of a diagnosis, not its end.

Illustrative scheme: The same house can, with a different commute, a different mode of travel and a different tax bill, produce a different life. Comparing greenery with a traffic jam is the same error as comparing a menu with the time at which dinner is eaten.

3. What error a ranking can conceal

A “good address” also depends on what the table leaves out. A missing park is easy to spot on a visit, but a long peak-hour commute may only become apparent after moving in. Changing nursery is usually easier than changing a mortgage, a workplace and the running of two cars.

In How’s Life? the OECD repeats that well-being has multiple dimensions and that an average conceals differences between groups and places.[5] It does not follow that Czech indexes have no value. It follows that an ORP ranking is a map of supply, not a verdict on how much waking time remains after the journey.

Type of failure What it looks like How to test it What limits the harm
Blind spot of time A top-ranked district, a long commute Door-to-door minutes at peak hours Count time as a negative column
Average instead of distribution “27 minutes” as if for everyone Who is above 45–60 min one way A histogram, not a single average
GDP instead of income A rich metropolis, a poorer hinterland GDP vs household disposable income Separate workplace and residence
Infrastructure instead of a service A station without a clock-face service The timetable against the shift Measure the service, not the track
Compensation illusion A larger house for a longer journey Sleep, blood pressure, family time Do not believe that the market will “even it out”

That also changes the question we are asking. We do not ask only which district wins the table. We ask which indicator will fail in a way the family will still see before signing. A creative team can tolerate ten bad rankings. A family with children cannot tolerate a single hidden loss of a hundred minutes a day.

When choosing housing, therefore, what decides alongside the score is whether journey time can be measured in advance, how hard the choice is to reverse, and how it will hit an ordinary day. A lower overall rank with a documented commute may be more suitable for a particular household than the ranking winner without that figure.

4. A ranking answers only the questions in the methodology

Composite indexes are needed because they replace part of impressions with a repeatable table. The problem arises the moment one ranking begins to stand for an entire life. In Compare Regions the CZSO publishes 34 indicators and does not say that the region at the top is the “happiest”. It shows how it stands on the chosen statistics.[1]

Every index contains hidden decisions. Who selected the indicators? How are they weighted? Is the unit a region, an ORP, or a municipality? Is the existence of a service measured, or the time to it? Is commuting counted as a cost? The answer to any of these questions can change the ranking.

Five facts without which a medal is only a publicity number

  • The territorial unit and the year of the data.
  • The list of indicators and the weights.
  • Whether it is supply, or accessibility in minutes.
  • Whether GDP is counted by workplace.
  • What the index expressly does not measure.

For buying a house, therefore, a small personal benchmark matters more than someone else’s overall ranking. Thirty actual journeys — in rain, at peak hours, with school, with delay — can say more than a thousand points for greenery in a district the family will see only at the weekend.

5. Compare columns, not district winners

The market in rankings can be described without a false podium of champions. The following map does not say who is the most pleasant to live with. It shows around what work the individual frameworks build their score and which failure the reader should look for on purpose.

The rows are not a judgment on the quality of the analysts. The same institution offers more than one table and each can change within a few years. The comparison is meant to prevent a different error: that a family takes over an excellent-looking wage column from a product that does not see its morning.

Interpretive scheme: The sides of the table are not a ranking of happiness. They separate supply, time, mode of travel and the tax bill.

6. Czech frameworks: what they measure and what time in the car misses

When reading methodologies the point is not “that the statisticians overslept”. The point is that a static territorial datum assumes that a person at point A has, without friction, the services and work of point B. Physical distance and the time barrier do not enter the formula, or enter only as the existence of a road and a station.

Different methodologies may measure access to a service, commuting duration or only the existence of infrastructure; these quantities cannot be swapped. The OECD framework works with work–life balance and historically used commuting as a secondary indicator, but a national comparison of countries is not a histogram of Czech satellites.[5][22] The CZSO tracks commuting in the census, separately from its 34-item Compare Regions application.[1][6][7]

It does not follow that the indexes should be abolished. It follows that their user is buying a map of infrastructure and socio-economics, not a stopwatch.

What needs to be tested

The first test is definition: does the column measure existence, distance, or peak-hour time? The second is the unit: a region may be green and your slip-road jammed. The third is the population: the average of the employed is not a parent with two children and one car. The fourth is reversibility: can the decision be repaired, or is it already in the land register?

For an individual who wants a rough orientation among regions, Compare Regions may be decisive. For a family the sentence “the district won” is not enough. It needs to know the minutes, the mode, the service and the boundary beyond which a person must travel the journey themselves, not read it.

7. The census sees commuting. It does not see your afternoon

The 2021 Population and Housing Census is the primary Czech source on commuting. In its publication on commuting to work and school and in the popularising text By car to work and school, the CZSO states that the car was the main means of transport for the journey to work for 60.2% of persons with a recorded means of transport (more than two million persons). Urban public transport accounted for 15.5% (530 thousand), walking 10.4% (356 thousand).[6][7] This is the main mode of the journey to work among those for whom the means was recorded. It is not the share of all residents of Czechia. It is not time in minutes.

On the census page on means of transport the CZSO further states that a car driven by the commuting person was used for the journey to work by almost 1.9 million persons, 54.8% of commuters to work with a recorded means; the difference from 60.2% is that the second figure includes the broader category “car as the main means”, while the first emphasises driving one’s own car.[8] This is not a contradiction between two truths. They are two definitions. A headline that turns them into “seven in ten Czechs drive every day” erases both.

The frequency of commuting to work in the census shows that most commuters with a recorded frequency travel at least five times a week; in Prague the share of daily journeys is lower than in the Zlín Region, where the CZSO reports 81.1% against 66.2% in Prague.[9] The census therefore sees regularity. It does not see whether a particular morning stood at 25 or 80 minutes.

The article therefore does not use the summary sentence “more than 3.5 million people commute daily”. Without a precise table and denominator it would mix the employed, pupils, work from home, an unrecorded workplace and different frequencies. What is documented are the shares above among persons with a recorded means of transport and the year 2021; this is not all residents of Czechia, nor the situation in 2026.[6][7][9]

8. An average of 27 minutes is not your journey

In an ad hoc module of the labour-force survey for 2019, Eurostat stated that the average commuting time of employed persons in Czechia was 27 minutes one way; the EU average was 25 minutes. Latvia had 33 minutes, Cyprus 19. Employed people living in suburbs and rural areas of the EU had on average a longer journey than city residents (31 against 24 minutes).[10] This is a European survey from 2019, one-way time, employed persons. It is not the satellite ring of Prague in 2026. It is not a distribution.

More important than the average is the tail. An average of 27 minutes can be produced by people who walk ten minutes and people who travel an hour. In a related report for 2019 Eurostat stated that most employed people in the EU travelled less than 30 minutes and that a smaller share travelled 60 minutes or more.[10] Czech satellites do not have their own row in this statistic. Anyone who wants to know the tail around Prague and Brno must reach for regional census data and transport models, not for a single European average.

It does not follow that 27 minutes is a lie. It follows that it is a map of the middle. A family that buys a house according to the middle may buy the tail.

9. Distance is not the same as fitness. Nor sleep

Time behind the wheel in a jam is not passive rest. Nor is it automatically a heart attack. Medical studies measure associations, not an individual’s fate.

Hoehner and colleagues in the American Journal of Preventive Medicine (2012) examined 4 297 adults in 12 Texas metropolitan counties in 2000–2007. Road-network commuting distance was negatively associated with physical activity and cardiorespiratory fitness and positively with BMI, waist circumference and blood pressure; after adjustment for activity and fitness, the relationship with higher blood pressure in particular remained significant among the metabolic indicators.[11] This is a cross-section of a selected American population with a medical examination, not Czech satellites. It does not say that “every 15 km kills”. It says that longer distance commuting by car in this sample went hand in hand with worse fitness and metabolic risk.

Hansson and colleagues in BMC Public Health (2011; 11:834) examined 21 088 employed people in Skåne. For public-transport commuting over 60 minutes the odds ratios of worse indicators (sleep, stress, vitality, health, absence) were in the range of roughly 1.2–1.6. For the car the relationship was rather convex: difficulties rose in the 30–60 minute band (OR roughly 1.2–1.4) and in the category over 60 minutes they were lower — the authors read this cautiously, not as evidence that a very long car journey is beneficial.[12] From this study one cannot derive a general claim of “30% more difficulties above 45 minutes”; it describes associations by mode and commuting time band in one Swedish region.

Stutzer and Frey in the Scandinavian Journal of Economics (2008) showed on German panels that people with longer commutes report lower life satisfaction, even though the classical model promises compensation in wages or housing. At an average one-way time of 22 minutes in their data this was a reduction in satisfaction on the order of 0.1 points on the scale used.[13] This is Germany and subjective satisfaction, not Czech ORP. It nevertheless changes the ranking question: a larger house need not be the offset that the economic model promised.

International comparison holds only where the definitions hold. Texas distance, Skåne time, German satisfaction and the Czech census mode are four maps. Assembling from them a single sentence about the “Czech satellite” without reservation is a synthesis the data do not carry. Assembling from them a warning that journey time belongs in thinking about quality of life is something the data do carry.

10. Time in the car is not leisure. It is a tax on community

In Bowling Alone Putnam links long car commuting with less room for clubs, neighbours and informal ties.[14] The book itself is not here a basis for a precise Czech effect “for every ten minutes”, and the article therefore does not take over such a percentage. The safe conclusion is more modest: journey time competes with other parts of the day; the size of the impact on community life in Czechia is not known from the cited source.

Walkable access to school is a different, more measurable layer of the same thing. Associations and initiatives that map children’s journeys show that the existence of a school building is not the same as a safe 15 minutes on foot.[15] An index that counts nursery capacity and does not count accompaniment by car measures an institution. It does not measure the morning.

Imagine two illustrations, not a report. A family in a regional city with an average index score: work 15 minutes by tram, school 500 metres, home at 16:30. A family in a well-rated district: two commutes of 60–80 minutes by car, return after 19.00. A high territorial score by itself does not say which family has the easier day. There are no names here; the example only shows why journey time should be a separate item in the decision.

11. A district’s GDP is not a household’s income

The absence of time in the index meets a second optical illusion. Gross domestic product in regional accounts is attributed to the place of economic activity, not the place of residence. For Prague in 2024 the CZSO reported GDP per inhabitant in purchasing-power parity of 192% of the EU average; the region thereby clearly leads domestically.[16] The public source therefore supports the value 192%, not a rounded “up to 200%”. The CZSO also publishes net household disposable income per inhabitant, which is closer to the living standard of the place of residence than GDP per head.[16][17]

It does not follow that Prague is not rich. It follows that regional GDP by place of work also includes the output of people who leave in the evening. From regional GDP, however, one cannot without further data infer the budget of a particular municipality or the impact of the budgetary assignment of taxes; that is a different accounting system and this article does not model it.

The optical illusion is therefore double. A quality index may praise a hinterland district for incomes and greenery. The GDP account may punish the same hinterland because the work is counted elsewhere. Time in the car joins both errors: a person produces value in the metropolis and spends life in a municipality that does not get a corresponding row in the GDP table.

12. A motorway and a station still do not determine the length of the journey

Before the motorway junction, people checked whether a municipality “has a road”. With the junction something more complicated is checked: whether the junction shortens time, or brings a jam to a ramp that used to be quiet. A junction is navigation to capacity. It is not automatic proof of accessibility.

One table can combine three true figures into an untrue conclusion. First: there is a station in the municipality. Second: wages in the region are rising. Third: crime is low. Each figure may fit, but the synthesis “living here means a short day” does not follow. Elsewhere an index uses a high-quality secondary environmental score even though a primary timetable is available.

A four-step check of accessibility

  1. Existence: the road, the track, the stop are there.
  2. Authority: the service is at shift time, not only on the map.
  3. Support: a concrete door-to-door minute at peak hours.
  4. Scope: it fits the school year, the shift, a second car, illness.

The most reliable working procedure therefore separates the map from the stopwatch. First a table of journeys, modes and delays is created. Only then is the ranking read. If the journey cannot be travelled in the advertised time, the claim is not promoted to quality of life merely because the district leads.

WHO recommendations on physical activity rest on the finding that regular movement reduces the risks of diseases of civilisation; passive sitting in a car does not replace this dose.[18] An IZA discussion of active commuting shows that walking and cycling can have a different health account from the car — again as an association, not as an order to move into the centre.[19] Infrastructure that enables an active mode changes the layer. Infrastructure that only speeds entry into a jam changes the place where one waits.

13. The price of a kilometre is the price of a raw material, not the price of a day

The property market uses several languages at once. The developer pays per metre. The bank the instalment. The driver fuel. The family time. The municipality the nursery. A direct comparison of house prices without knowledge of the daily journey therefore misleads.

A cheap house beyond the city can be economically excellent if the commute is short or the rail service clock-face. The same house can be expensive for a task that both parents repeat twice a day, consume two cars and in the end surrender sleep. An expensive flat by the tram can be pointless for someone who wants a garden and has work in the municipality. It can be cheaper for a decision with a high price of time if it reduces the number of hours that cannot be bought back.

The true price of an accepted day

Instalment + energy + two cars + fuel + parking + minutes × working days + lost sleep + the price of delay + future unsaleability.

A day is cheap only when, after the journey, time remains that could be used.

Costs also include fluctuation. If the same journey once in five days stretches to double, the average price of a day is not the price of an ideal morning. If the peak-hour service does not arrive, the cost is a second car. If life is firmly tied to a single ramp, the cost is also leaving.

It is therefore useful to measure the price per accepted weekday, not the price per metre. Accepted means that the day met predetermined criteria: work, school, sleep, and did not cause a subsequent correction of the daily regime that the ranking does not see.

14. What to count for which question

A reasonable choice does not start with a district medal, but with the type of question and the control mechanism. The following recommendations are not a ranking of municipalities. They are a signpost to the first test. For each category it makes sense to choose two columns and compare them on the same journey, at the same time and with the same budget of minutes.

Need First group of evidence Decisive test Unacceptable shortcut
Rough orientation among regions CZSO Compare Regions Year and definition of the indicator Buy a house according to the region
Services in the municipality Obce v datech, MMR ORP Existence vs the minute Travel to a doctor as the journey to work
Daily work Census mode + one’s own stopwatch Door-to-door at peak hours The European average of 27 min
Health account Hoehner, Hansson, WHO — with a population caveat Mode and time band, not fate “The study proves your heart attack”
Tax account of the hinterland GDP vs disposable income Workplace vs residence Prague 192% of the EU as a wage in the satellite
Children to school Walking map, timetable 15 minutes without a car? Nursery capacity without the journey

For Czech use a separate peak-hour test is necessary. An English commuting survey will not measure whether a service will catch a shift in an industrial zone, whether a pram fits on the bus in winter, or whether “27 minutes” was ever true on your ramp. The test set should include rain, an accident on the D1, a school trip and a day on which the right answer is “today it cannot be done by car”.

Some households in the end need more than one map. One for wages, a second for school, a third for the service, a fourth for tax. That is not a failure of moving. It is the same decision as using a different tool for a mortgage and a different one for a timetable. The condition is that the division is intelligible and people know where the final stopwatch arises.

15. A short test that says more than an ORP ranking

A choice can be made without a six-month move and without an impulse to buy the ranking winner. A short, disciplined week is enough. Its aim is not to prove that a district can do something impressive in an infographic. The aim is to find situations in which the day fails, and to see whether the family can bear them.

Three tests before moving

The first is about time: how many door-to-door minutes on a Wednesday at eight in the morning and at five in the afternoon, not on Saturday at ten? The second is about mode: is there a service that will carry the shift without a second car? The third is about the body: what remains of sleep and dinner when the journey stretches twice a week? A product that answers a point earlier in the table need not be better if, a year later, nobody can find out why the days look different.

Before signing

  • Time five working journeys in both directions.
  • Record the mode, delay and the price of fuel or the ticket.
  • Separate travel to a doctor from travel to work.
  • Check school and nursery at the time they actually start.
  • Calculate how many hours a year 90 minutes a day times 220 shifts is.

A pilot should also have drop-out conditions. For example: a single journey that regularly eats sleep below six hours, the absence of a service after 19.00, or the need for two cars where the budget counted on one. A high greenery score should not override an error whose impact the household cannot accept.

The last output is not only the name of the winning ORP. It is an operating rule: what the index is used for, what it is not used for, which time must be travelled, what must be approved by the person who will drive, and when the decision will be opened again. Rankings, timetables and motorway ramps change. Without a review date even a good moving plan gradually turns into a family superstition.

16. A bedroom can subsidise a metropolis. The index need not see it

The last layer joins transport to the account. People live in a municipality that wins on greenery and work in a city that wins on GDP. The CZSO methodologically acknowledges this loop in the regional accounts by the fact that GDP and household disposable income are not the same.[16][17] On intra-country commuting between regions Eurostat shows that millions of Europeans regularly work elsewhere than they live; Czechia is not an exception to the mechanism, even if the exact counts depend on the definition of the region.[20]

An index that adds up wages at the place of residence and doctors at the place of residence may praise the satellite. It does not ask how many of those wages were created sixty minutes away and how many of those doctors suffice in the evening when the family returns at seven. The Ministry of Regional Development and spatial planning can name suburbanisation as pressure on service provision.[2][21] Without a time column, however, the naming remains without a stopwatch.

OECD regional well-being and Czech Compare Regions are useful maps.[1][22] They are not a verdict on whether it is worth getting up at 5:15. That decision can be borne only by a day that someone has actually travelled. An index can help select candidates. The journey must be done by work — in the literal sense.

17. The price of housing also includes the everyday journey

Rankings join figures on services, environment and the economy into a single order. A housing decision, however, also includes the everyday ferrying of children, the journey to work and time that may not have its own column in the table.

That is why the contest is not only for the best score. It is a contest for daily context. For the ramp, for the service, for the habit of getting up at 5:15. The more comfortable the house that arises in the table, the higher the price of leaving can be. A mortgage, a school and two cars are hard to export.

Space and quiet beyond the city can be a fundamental advantage for a household. Before buying, however, it is advisable to verify actual peak-hour time, the timetable, an alternative to the car and how the week works if one vehicle is out of action.

A suitable address for a particular household need not lead the overall index. It must match the actual daily regime, the budget and tolerance of commuting.

The moment the ranking only ranks greenery, we are choosing a map. The moment we buy a house by it, build a timetable and surrender sleep, we are also choosing a new structure of the day. And then the question is no longer: “Which district won?”

Let us ask: How many minutes will the actual peak-hour journey show, and is this price acceptable even when the ranking has not counted it?
