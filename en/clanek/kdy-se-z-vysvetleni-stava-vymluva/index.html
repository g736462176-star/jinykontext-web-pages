When Does an Explanation Become an Excuse?

Understanding the causes of human behavior does not mean approving the act. Where is the line between an explanation, a mitigating circumstance, an excuse, and responsibility?

Someone attacks another person. The report is brief and the moral judgment swift. Then more information emerges: he grew up in an environment where violence was normal. Someone threatened him before the attack. He was surrounded by a group that escalated the conflict. He had barely slept for weeks. And suddenly a sentence appears that reliably divides the room.

“That does not excuse him.”

Maybe not. But we often say this before anyone has offered an excuse. Someone was merely explaining why something happened.

And this is where one of the most interesting conflicts between psychology and morality arises: the better we understand the causes of human behavior, the more we sometimes feel that we are “taking responsibility away” from a person.

An explanation answers the question “why.” An excuse answers the question “to what extent should we hold the person responsible for it?” They are two different operations.

The philosophy of moral responsibility has long distinguished between mere causal responsibility — the person caused the outcome — and responsibility in the moral sense, which is typically connected to questions of control, intention, the ability to understand what one is doing, and knowledge of relevant circumstances.1 Research in the psychology of moral judgment likewise shows that people combine information about causal role, intention, circumstances, and outcome when assessing blame.2

Four statements that sound similar — but are not the same

Imagine a hypothetical situation: a man strikes another person.

Several statements can be made about the event.

Explanation“He struck him because he was afraid.” It describes a mechanism or motive. By itself, it does not determine the degree of responsibility.
Mitigating circumstance“His ability to assess the situation calmly was limited.” Responsibility remains, but its moral weight may change.
Excuse“Under these circumstances, he lacked relevant control or knowledge.” The act may still be wrong, but the agent’s blame is reduced or disappears.
Justification“Under these circumstances, the act was permissible.” We are not merely changing our assessment of the person; we are changing our assessment of the act itself.

These categories are not a universal vocabulary of criminal law. They are analytical and philosophical distinctions that help us understand the problem. In theories of responsibility, there is a fundamental difference between the claim „what he did was wrong, but cannot be fully attributed to him“ and the claim „under the circumstances, it was not wrong“.3

In everyday debate, these levels often blur together. As soon as someone mentions a traumatic past, manipulation, coercion, or limited information, the other side hears: “So he is innocent.”

But there is a vast space between “100 percent guilty” and “bears no responsibility.”

A cause is not a defense

Every human act has causes.

This is almost a banal statement. People do not wake up as beings disconnected from their biology, past, environment, social ties, and immediate situation. Psychology, after all, studies precisely the relationship between behavior, the brain, and the environment.

Yet when we describe a cause of wrongdoing, something in us resists.

If we say someone stole money because of a debt, it sounds like an excuse.

If we say someone succumbed to group pressure, it sounds like removing personal responsibility.

If we say aggression was shaped by a learned social strategy, someone may hear: “it was not his fault.”

But that does not follow logically.

A causal explanation can be completely true while leaving moral responsibility virtually unchanged.

For example, a desire for money explains a robbery. It does not automatically excuse it. Jealousy may explain an attack. It is not an excuse. Anger may explain an insult. It says nothing definitive about whether the person was able to control himself.

An explanation begins to bear on responsibility only when it provides information relevant to what the person knew, intended, and controlled, and what real options for action were available to him.

The boundary is often called control

Imagine two situations.

In the first, someone signs a fraudulent contract because they will be paid for it.

In the second, they sign the same contract because someone credibly threatens immediate violence against their child.

The physical movement of the hand is the same. The signature is the same. The outcome may be the same.

But our moral intuition changes dramatically.

Why? Because the circumstance is no longer merely a cause. It affects how much the act was truly “up to him.”

Philosophical theories of blame commonly regard some degree of control, practical reasoning, and moral understanding of the situation as relevant to accountability. Coercion, ignorance, or certain forms of manipulation can weaken responsibility precisely by affecting these conditions.3

Experimental research also shows that coercion changes more than how others assess the agent. In laboratory studies, social coercion also reduced people’s subjective sense of authorship over their own actions — their so-called sense of agency.4

Interpret with caution: a lower subjective sense of control does not automatically mean lower moral or legal responsibility. The experiment shows a psychological effect of coercion on the experience of one’s own activity; the normative conclusion is a separate question.

What did the person know?

The second major pillar is knowledge.

If a person presses a button knowing it will harm someone, we assess them differently from someone who was reasonably told that the button would merely turn on a light.

In the literature on moral responsibility, this area is called the epistemic condition: the extent to which a person knew or should have known what they were doing and what the relevant consequences were.5

There is no simple switch here either.

They did not know — because the information could not be discovered?

They did not know — because they chose not to find out?

They did not know — because they ignored an obvious risk?

They did not know — because someone actively deceived them?

Ignorance is present in all four statements. Their moral significance may be completely different.

“I did not know” describes a state. Only the question “why did you not know?” can tell us something about responsibility.

Intention is not everything. But it changes almost everything.

Two drivers make the same mistake and both hit a pedestrian.

The first intended to hit the pedestrian.

The second did not see him.

The outcome is the same for the victim. The moral assessment of the agents is not.

Research in the psychology of moral judgment repeatedly shows that intention, causal role, and outcome enter our assessments through different routes. A review of moral decision-making in Annual Review of Psychology emphasizes that judging an act as wrong and judging a person as blameworthy are not the same thing.2

This is crucial to our question as well.

Information about a person’s childhood may explain why they react aggressively. But if, at that moment, they deliberately planned harm, had sufficient control, and understood the consequences, that information may not substantially change their responsibility.

Conversely, information that directly changes our conclusion about intention, control, or knowledge may be morally decisive.

Why the outcome sometimes misleads us

Now for a small experiment.

Two people make an equally dangerous mistake.

The first throws a cigarette butt into dry grass. By chance, it rains and nothing happens.

The second does the same. The wind carries a spark into the woods and a fire starts.

They acted equally recklessly. One of them was lucky.

Yet we tend to judge the second person more harshly. In philosophy and psychology, this problem is called moral luck — moral luck. Research shows that consequences can influence judgments of blame even when part of the difference arose by chance.6

This is an important reminder, because we sometimes mistake an explanation for an excuse simply because the outcome made us angry.

When the harm is small, we hear the context.

When the harm is catastrophic, the same context may feel like an insult to the victim.

Emotionally, this is understandable. Analytically, however, the question remains the same: which part of the outcome was actually under the person’s control?

“But other people with similar childhoods did not do it.”

That is a strong objection — and often a valid one.

A statistical or psychological association is not destiny.

If a particular life experience increases the likelihood of a certain behavior, that does not mean everyone with that experience will act the same way. Human action arises from a combination of dispositions, situation, learning, social relationships, opportunities, and decisions.

That is precisely why it would be wrong to say:

“He grew up this way, so he could not have acted differently.”

But the opposite shortcut can be just as wrong:

“Someone else handled the same experience, so his past has no explanatory significance.”

A single counterexample does not disprove a causal influence. It merely reminds us that causal influence is not a deterministic verdict.

A useful filter: instead of asking “is this an excuse?” ask: “Does this information change anything about intention, knowledge, control, or the options that were realistically available?” If not, it may be psychologically interesting while being almost morally irrelevant.

Why we prefer to see character rather than situation

Psychology knows a familiar tendency called fundamental attribution error: when explaining other people’s behavior, we tend to overestimate their stable personal traits and underestimate situational influences.7

Someone behaves aggressively → they are an aggressive person.

Someone fails → they are incompetent.

Someone lies → they are a liar.

These conclusions may sometimes be correct. They simply arise faster than an analysis of the circumstances.

Context is psychologically uncomfortable because it complicates the clean story of character. Once we admit situation, pressure, lack of information, or chance, the moral map stops being black and white.

And this is where the fear arises that explanation “relativizes everything.”

If we explain everything, does blame disappear?

No.

This may be the most important point of the entire article.

A complete causal explanation of an act is not the same as a complete moral excuse.

In principle, we can know almost everything about a person: their childhood, biology, social environment, fears, relationships, motives, and the pressure they faced at that moment. And still conclude that they had the relevant capacity to understand the situation and direct their own actions.

Understanding can increase compassion while leaving responsibility intact.

This possibility is also developed in the literature on “responsibility without blame,” which distinguishes holding someone responsible in a practical sense from certain forms of condemning, hostile attitudes toward them.8

So we do not have to choose between only two statements:

“He is a monster.”

or

“He is not responsible for anything.”

There can be a third:

“I understand how he got here. And he is still responsible for what he did.”

So when does an explanation really become an excuse?

Not when we describe a cause.

It becomes one when, without sufficient reason, we turn a cause into a conclusion of lesser responsibility.

For example:

“He was upset, so he is not responsible for the attack.”

The first part may be true. The second does not follow from it.

Or:

“He grew up around violence, so he had no other choice.”

The past may be a relevant cause. A claim that there was no choice requires a much stronger argument.

An excuse is therefore often not the circumstance itself. It is an unwarranted leap between two levels:

“This contributed to his action” → “therefore we should not hold him responsible for it.”

The exact opposite error works the same way:

“He is still responsible” → “therefore we do not need to examine his past, pressure, or circumstances.”

Both shortcuts destroy understanding.

Why we need explanations even when they excuse no one

There is another practical reason why it is dangerous to dismiss causes as “excuses.”

If we want to prevent a problem from recurring, moral judgment is not enough.

If we say only that the perpetrator was a bad person, we have a category. We do not have a mechanism.

If we want to reduce violence, organizational errors, fraud, traffic accidents, or recidivism, we need to know which conditions contribute to action — even if we still hold the individual responsible for their choice.

An explanation is not merely a tool of compassion.

It is a tool of prevention.

Moral judgment may tell us whom we condemn. A causal explanation may tell us what we must change to keep it from happening again.

Understanding is not the same as forgiving

Perhaps we are afraid of explanations because we hear forgiveness in them.

When we begin to examine fear, the past, social pressure, ignorance, or limited control, it can feel as though we are slowly distancing the person from their act.

But a more precise view can do the opposite.

It can separate what the person is truly responsible for from what merely resembles responsibility.

It can stop us from punishing someone for chance they did not control.

And at the same time stop us from turning every cause into an alibi.

So perhaps the best question is not:

“Is this an explanation or an excuse?”

But:

“What exactly does this information change about what the person knew, intended, and could control?”

Because understanding why someone did something wrong does not mean saying they were entitled to do it.

And acknowledging their responsibility does not mean we must stop understanding how they came to bear it.
