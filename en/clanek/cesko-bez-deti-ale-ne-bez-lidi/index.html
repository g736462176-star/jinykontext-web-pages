Czechia with Fewer Children, but Not Necessarily Fewer People: The Role of Migration

Without migration, Czechia is shrinking through natural change. Birth rates, the foreign population, temporary protection, and ageing cannot be treated as separate stories.

People, demography & migration

Czechia gained inhabitants in 2025. Not because more people were born than died, but because net migration outweighed a record natural decrease. The war in Ukraine accelerated this story. It did not create it. Migration is no longer a temporary deviation in Czech demography, even though some residence statuses remain temporary.

Methodological note: The article distinguishes the population stock, annual flows, citizenship, residence status, and employment registrations. The figures for 2025, the first quarter of 2026, and the number of foreign nationals on 30 June 2026 are not one common snapshot and must not be added without explanation.

1. The Year the Population Grew While Czechia Shrunk from Within

At the end of 2025, Czechia had 6,339 more inhabitants than a year earlier. At first sight, the country appears to have grown. Once the balance is separated, another picture emerges: 77,636 children were born, 113,337 people died, and natural change therefore reduced the population by 35,701. A total of 110,600 people moved into the country and 68,560 moved out. Net migration was 42,040—enough to offset the natural decrease and leave a small overall increase.[1]

The population equation for 2025
  • −35,701: natural change, births minus deaths.
  • +42,040: international net migration, arrivals minus departures.
  • +6,339: total population change.

It is therefore wrong to write that “42,000 migrants arrived”. The balance is the difference between two flows and includes people who left. The stock of registered foreign nationals is yet another quantity, different from the number of arrivals in one year. Mixing these figures produces the fastest and least accurate conclusions.

2. Fewer Births Are Not Only the Result of Smaller Generations

In 2021, 111,793 children were born in Czechia. In 2025 the number was 77,636, approximately 31 percent lower. Over the same period, the total fertility rate fell from 1.83 to 1.28 children per woman. This is a synthetic indicator: it represents the number of children a woman would have if she passed through her entire reproductive life at the age-specific rates of that calendar year. It is not the final number of children that real generations of women will ultimately have.[2]

The Czech Statistical Office notes that there were almost 36,000 fewer women aged 26 to 33 than in 2021. That explains part of the decline. Age-specific fertility rates also fell, however. It is therefore not enough to say that “the large cohorts have passed”. The latest data also reflect changes in whether, when, and under what conditions people decide to start a family.

Demography does not tell us why each individual makes that decision. It shows only the combined result of millions of different decisions. Family policy is therefore poorly served by reducing a statistical decline to one universal cause.

3. Natural Decrease Began Before the War

The year 2022 brought an exceptional migration surge, not the first positive balance. Natural change had already become negative in 2019. The decrease has continued since then and reached its lowest value in this series in 2025. Net migration was positive in 2018–2021, before Russia’s invasion of Ukraine.

This is an important counterweight to two simplifications. The first says that the war created Czechia’s demographic situation. The second treats 2022 as an ordinary annual increase likely to repeat. Neither is true. The war changed the size and composition of migration, but demand for labour and family migration existed before it.[3]

4. From a Marginal Minority to More Than One Tenth of the Population

The number of registered foreign nationals rose from about 200,000 around 2000 to 467,600 in 2015 and 660,800 in 2021. After the wartime jump in 2022, it has remained above 1.1 million. On 30 June 2026, the Ministry of the Interior registered 1,140,522 foreign nationals, equivalent to 10.47 percent of the population.

The time series contains a methodological break. From 2001, the statistics include a broader range of long-term stays than the earlier records. Any chart must mark this change so that a viewer does not mistake an expanded definition for actual growth.[4]

5. Foreign National, Migrant, Refugee, and Temporary-Protection Holder Are Not Synonyms

A foreign national is a person without Czech citizenship. In demographic terms, a migrant is someone associated with a cross-border move or change of residence. A recognised refugee has received asylum through an individual procedure. A holder of temporary protection is covered by a collective emergency regime used by the European Union during mass displacement.

One person may be both a foreign national and a migrant, but the words do not describe the same characteristic. Someone who moved to Czechia and later acquired Czech citizenship is no longer present in the foreign-national statistics. Net migration also includes both foreign citizens and Czech citizens moving into or out of the country.

An accurate text therefore always says whether it describes a number of people at a particular date, a number of events during a year, or a number of active registrations.

6. Ukraine Dominates the Data but Does Not Explain All Migration

At the end of June 2026, the Ministry of the Interior recorded the largest group as citizens of Ukraine, at 614,330. They were followed by citizens of Slovakia (127,133), Vietnam (70,194), Russia (37,416), Romania (21,294), and the Philippines (17,437). This ranking describes citizenship, not ethnicity, country of birth, or reason for arriving.

The same snapshot divided residence into 342,707 temporary permits, 407,003 permanent permits, and 390,812 active temporary-protection registrations. These figures must not be added to another protection register without checking whether they are already included in the total foreign-national count.

7. A Temporary Status Can Last for Years

The word temporary describes a legal regime, not a guaranteed length of stay. A holder of temporary protection can work, attend school, rent a home, and plan several years of life in the country granting that status. Today’s registration still cannot tell us whether a person will stay, return, leave for another country, or move into another residence category.

At the end of June 2026, Czechia had 390,812 active temporary-protection registrations. The cumulative number of protections ever issued would be a different and much larger quantity. Public debate should work with the active stock and always state the date.

8. A Negative Balance in the First Quarter of 2026 Is Not Yet a Reversal

In the first quarter of 2026, the population fell by 19,836. Natural change was −12,582 and net migration −7,254. The Czech Statistical Office also pointed to a high number of expired temporary-protection registrations. An administrative end to a registration need not be identical to the moment a person physically crossed the border.

One quarter cannot be compared mechanically with the whole of 2025. The negative figure is a real result for that statistical period, but not yet evidence of a lasting return to negative migration. Establishing that would require a longer series and an understanding of changes in registration practices.[5]

9. Migration Primarily Changes the Working-Age Population

The age profile of foreign nationals is younger than that of the Czech population as a whole. In 2024, 80.7 percent were aged 15–64, 13.5 percent were aged 0–14, and 5.7 percent were 65 or older. In the short term, this can reduce pressure on the labour market and public finances.

It does not mean that every arrival automatically fills a skilled vacancy. Recognition of qualifications, language, working conditions, housing, and the possibility of bringing family members determine whether an arrival becomes a lasting part of society.

In the public MPSV data set, the qualified estimate for February 2025 was 849,049 employment relationships involving foreign nationals. One person may hold several jobs, so this is not a count of unique individuals. It describes the volume of registered relationships, not integration by itself. MPSV also warns that figures from July 2024 onward are qualified estimates because source systems are being changed.[6]

10. “A Foreigner’s Child” Can Mean Three Different Things

For 2025, three different shares of births can be calculated depending on the definition: about 7,200 children with foreign citizenship, 10,000 children born to a foreign-national mother, and 13,100 children with at least one foreign-national parent. Out of all 77,636 births, these were approximately 9, 13, and 17 percent respectively.

The difference exists because a child with one Czech parent may acquire Czech citizenship even when the other parent is foreign. Statistics should therefore never say “children of foreigners” without defining the term. The Czech Statistical Office also reports that in 2025 the average fertility rate of foreign women was lower than that of Czech women. Migration does not automatically restore a high birth rate.[7]

11. Migration Buys Time but Does Not Stop Ageing

At the end of 2025, people aged 0–14 made up 15.1 percent of the population, those aged 15–64 made up 64.1 percent, and people aged 65 or over made up 20.8 percent. There were roughly 138 seniors for every 100 children. The younger profile of part of the foreign population improves this structure but does not reverse it.

Migrants also age, leave, acquire citizenship, form families, and need schools, housing, and health care. For municipalities, the key questions are therefore not only how many people arrive, but where they live and how long they remain.

12. The Official Projection Treats Migration as a Basic Parameter

The Czech Statistical Office’s 2023 medium projection assumes net migration of about 35,000 a year from 2028 and a total fertility rate of around 1.5. This is a conditional scenario. Actual fertility in 2025 was 1.28, so no future population figure can be quoted as a certainty.

According to the projection scenarios, the share of people aged 65 and over is set to rise to roughly 29–30 percent around mid-century even with positive migration. The projection therefore does not say that migration solves the problem. It shows the scale of migration required in a particular scenario to prevent an even faster population decline.[8]

13. A Permanent Phenomenon Needs Permanent Rules

Receiving a person is a one-off event. Integration is a multi-year process. It includes language, school, housing, work, recognition of qualifications, health care, legal certainty, and a relationship to place. If the state addresses only the moment of arrival, it leaves the most expensive work to municipalities, schools, employers, and families.

Long-term policy must therefore answer five questions: Which legal migration routes does Czechia want to offer? How quickly will it recognise qualifications? Who will fund language training and local capacity? How should transitions from temporary statuses work? And how will integration be measured without confusing citizenship, origin, and residence status?

Migration Is Not Merely a Labour-Market Tool

Employment figures often conceal the fact that arrivals do not live in a spreadsheet of vacancies. They come with partners, children, parents, or a plan that changes over several years. A worker filling a shortage occupation today may tomorrow need a school, paediatrician, home, and a chance to retrain. If policy watches only work-permit counts, it measures an entry point rather than actual integration.

The difference is visible at municipal level. A small town may gain pupils because several families arrive, while also needing new language support. An industrial town can gain workers and at the same time face pressure on rental housing, transport, and primary care. That pressure is not an argument against migration. It is an argument for planning arrivals together with the capacity of the places in which people settle.

It is equally misleading to divide arrivals into “temporary” and “permanent” according to their first stamp. A person with temporary protection may work in the country, their child may attend a Czech school, and their family may build ties that cannot be cancelled by one administrative deadline. Status is a legal category, not a forecast of a life. Transitions between statuses and actual departures therefore matter as much as the number of permits issued.[9]

What Happens If Arrivals Leave

The sensitivity of the demographic balance to migration is clearest in a simple scenario. If net migration were halved while the natural decrease stayed the same, the positive overall balance in 2025 would no longer have covered the difference between births and deaths. This is not a forecast but a dependency test: it shows that population growth is not self-sustaining and that any change in migration flows quickly reaches the total.

For schools, employers, and municipalities, age and length of stay matter more than an annual national balance. Working-age arrivals can ease labour shortages in the short term, but they also create future demand for homes and services. If they stay longer, they become part of the country’s age structure. If they leave, unused capacity, interrupted education, or an employer who planned on an optimistic assumption may remain.

Policy therefore needs a multi-year outlook: how many people are expected in each region, which occupations are short, which schools and surgeries have room, and how transitions among residence, work, and family life will be funded. Without it, migration remains an improvised repair for each new shortage.

Statistics Must Keep Track of Generations

The largest error is to infer future births or automatic population renewal from the number of foreign nationals. Children born to women with different residence statuses may have Czech citizenship, foreign citizenship, or ties to both. Each definition answers a different question. For school planning, the relevant child is the one living in the municipality; for citizenship statistics, citizenship matters; and for the health system, actual use of care matters.

A good demographic debate therefore always gives the population, period, and definition. Only then can it compare changes in births, parents’ age, the size of migration flows, or household composition. One large number without this trio looks precise but may describe several incomparable realities.

Arrivals Are More Than Working Age

Public debate sometimes describes migration as a supply of “hands”. That language ignores the fact that working age is not the only relevant characteristic. Arrivals have children, partners, and parents. Some work in highly qualified occupations; others work in physically demanding sectors where their health and capacity can change quickly. Long-term planning therefore needs the full age profile, not just the number employed.

Families also change local demand. A child in a Czech school is not a “cost of migration” detached from society; they are a pupil for whom the municipality must provide a teacher, after-school care, and sometimes an assistant. A worker without accessible housing cannot stably fill the vacancy assigned to them in the statistics. Investment in integration is not charity added to the economy. It is a condition for the economic contribution to last beyond one employment contract.

Three Timelines That Must Not Be Collapsed

Demography works with three timelines at once. The first is the immediate flow: who arrived and who left in a given year. The second is medium-term residence: who stayed long enough to use schools, health care, and housing. The third is generational change: how many children were born, under what conditions, and whether their families stayed. One figure cannot stand in for all three processes.

If arrivals increase, the first-year effect may be mainly labour-market related. A few years later, demand for homes and schools appears. Only later still do we see whether stable households formed or people moved elsewhere. Each stage needs different indicators and different policy. Confusing them means responding to the past after the problem has already changed.

Czechia therefore does not need another argument over one magic number. It needs a regularly published dashboard placing migration beside fertility, age, region, length of stay, school capacity, and economic activity. Only then can we tell whether society is changing permanently or merely offsetting one statistical row with another for a short time.

Time also matters in political language. “Fewer will arrive” may describe the next quarter, while “they will be part of society” describes the next generation. Both can be true at once. Responsible analysis therefore neither promises that one migration year will solve a long decline in births nor pretends that every temporary figure already represents a permanent shift.

The decisive issue will not be only how many people arrive, but whether they can live safely, work at a level matching their qualifications, send children to school, and move naturally between regions. Integration that narrows these possibilities creates an unstable workforce and makes departure more likely. Integration that broadens them turns arrivals into neighbours, colleagues, and parents of classmates. That process is slower than issuing a permit, but demographically more important.

There is also a difference between the national and regional pictures. The total population can grow while some municipalities lose young families and others rapidly gain working-age residents. For schools, transport, and health care, the national average is only a frame. Decisions must use the place where people actually live, not merely where a register records them.

Migration does not answer why fewer children are born. It answers a different question: why the population is not yet falling even faster.
